I already have abs. What I care about now is keeping the weight off and thinking about my health over the long term. In this video I walk through the research that changed how I think about those two things and explain the decision I made for myself.

Most of the research I bring up in the video was done in people with obesity or an existing medical condition. That's important, especially if you're watching this and you already have abs. The studies can tell you what happened to the people in them. They don't tell you whether someone with your health history would see the same benefits.

## Why I wanted to get lean in the first place

I wanted abs because I wanted to look better. I knew what I wanted to see in the mirror, and getting there mattered to me. I also cared about what carrying extra weight might mean for my health. Those were two reasons to get serious about the same thing.

Early in the video, I talk about a study of people with prediabetes and a long follow-up study of people with severe obesity. I put these examples first because a lot of the conversation about getting lean starts and ends with appearance. What caught my attention in those studies were outcomes the researchers tracked over years. They were looking at people's lives well after the initial weight change.

That doesn't mean you can read your future health from the mirror. Visible abs don't tell you what your blood tests look like or whether you have a sleep problem. They were a goal I could see and work toward. The health questions deserve their own look.

## Why I stopped comparing myself with everybody around me

A lot of guys decide their weight is fine because they're thinner than their friends. I understand how easy it is to make that comparison. You see the same people every day, and whatever's common in that group starts to feel like the baseline.

Being thinner than the next guy tells you how you compare with him. It says very little about what's healthy for you. In the video I use abs to make the point because that's something my audience and I talk about all the time. It's also a goal I picked for myself. If weight or waist size is a concern, there's more to go on than how you look next to somebody else. A clinician can help put those numbers alongside your own history and health measurements.

## What I looked for in the studies

The part that got me interested was that the researchers were looking at more than pounds. One study I discuss involved people with obesity and prediabetes. Another looked at breathing interruptions during sleep in people with obesity and sleep apnea. Other results came from people with specific heart or metabolic conditions. These were different groups, with different reasons to be in each study.

If you're watching the research section, keep track of who was studied and what the researchers counted as a benefit. A study about breathing interruptions gives you an answer about breathing interruptions in the people who enrolled. It doesn't settle whether a healthy guy who sleeps well has anything to gain. A change in a group of people with a diagnosed condition doesn't automatically carry over to everybody.

I say in the video that the research was enough to influence what I decided to do. That's my interpretation. The question I get from guys who already have abs is more specific, and the evidence I discuss leaves that question open. There's a difference between being interested in a result and knowing that it applies to you.

## What changed when I reached my goal

By the time I had abs, the question I was asking had changed. I knew what it took to get down to the weight I wanted. What I didn't want was to treat reaching that weight like I was finished and then have to start over.

That's why I bring up a maintenance study in the second half of the video. I was interested in what happened after people had already lost weight. The groups in the study had different experiences with regain, and that got my attention because it was about the stage I was in. A plan that helped somebody get started was only part of the question. I wanted to know what happened after the initial result.

In the last chapter I talk about my own routine and what it cost me at the time. Those are details about my experience. If you're considering a change to your own routine, you can bring the research and your questions to a clinician who knows your history. My circumstances can't answer what makes sense for you.
