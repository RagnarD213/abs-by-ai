A 5 minute workout done every day produces better results for beginners than a 1 hour workout done twice per week.

And no, it will not be overtraining. You probably would be overtraining, and you couldn't make yourself do it, if you did an hour long workout every day. But a short 5 minute workout every day is actually easier than doing long workouts twice per week. No matter who you are, no matter your experience, you can do 5 minutes every day.

## What the research says, and why the real world is different

What I'm telling you right now is at odds with the scientific research. Essentially, the research says that with the same total workout time, the same total sets, and the same total weight lifted, people get essentially the same results in perfect laboratory conditions.

So looking at that, you might think, why not just pack it all into one long day every week and get it all done?

I've found that training beginners in the real world is totally different than a lab. In the real world, people skip their workouts. Sometimes things come up. Sometimes there's emergencies. Sometimes they can't make it to the gym. Sometimes they just don't feel like going, so they don't go.

In the real world, short daily workouts are better than long workouts done rarely.

## 4 reasons a 5 minute daily workout beats longer workouts

### Reason 1: No soreness, so no dread

Let's say you're not training at all right now. You're not doing any cardio, you're not doing any weightlifting. And then you go straight into an intense one hour workout. You are going to be sore the next day, and you're going to be exhausted for the next few days after that.

That feeling is going to make you associate pain with working out. Every time you think about the next workout, you're going to dread it. For the first month, you might force yourself with discipline to do it anyway. But eventually the discipline wears down, and that's when I see people start skipping.

On the other hand, if you just start doing five minutes at home, you'll barely feel the soreness at all. So when you wake up the next day, you're not going to be dreading your workout, and you'll do it again.

### Reason 2: There's no decision to make

In the real world, things come up. And if you're making a decision each time about whether you're working out or not, sometimes you'll make the wrong decision.

I experienced this firsthand when I used to work out three days a week. An emergency would come up on Thursday, and I'd say "I'll just do it tomorrow." But then tomorrow came and I didn't do it. Then it was "I'll start again next week." And the week was lost.

Now there is no decision. I work out every single day, even if I'm sick or injured. If I'm injured, I just modify the workout around the injury.

### Reason 3: A short workout fits in the morning

The morning is the best time to work out. But if you work a nine-to-five job, getting in a long workout in the morning is unrealistic. Five minutes in the morning is realistic.

### Reason 4: It becomes your identity

A short daily workout builds your identity as the type of person who works out every day no matter what.

It's kind of like brushing your teeth. You brush your teeth every day, and you don't think about whether today is a good day to do it or not. Once you're working out every day, working out will be just like that. You don't even think about it, you just do it.

## The best and worst time to work out

First of all, you have to plan your daily time to do it. The absolute best time to work out is the morning. If you're just starting with five minutes, you can wake up a little earlier and get it in.

But I know some of you guys are already waking up crazy early to go to a job. No problem. The second best time to work out is immediately after work. Go straight from your job to the gym, or straight from your job into your home workout area. Don't sit on the couch first. In my experience, most people who go home and take a break don't come back.

The absolute worst time to work out is late at night. And I say this as someone who used to work out late at night. It's going to disrupt your sleep. And you're a lot more likely to skip, because so many things can happen throughout the day.

## How to step it up month by month

Month one, do a five minute bodyweight workout at home every day. Most people underestimate commute time. If you're doing a five minute workout but you've got to drive 20 minutes each way to the gym, it's really a 45 minute time commitment. At home, it's five minutes.

Month two, step it up to ten minutes. You can simply double the five minute workout. Or you can buy a few basic pieces of equipment: a kettlebell, a yoga mat, an ab wheel, a [jump rope](/videos/how-to-jump-rope-without-tripping-skip-like-a-boxer/), and push-up handles.

Month three, start doing 15 minute workouts and keep using that same basic equipment.

Month four, invest in a home gym setup or start going to a commercial gym. At this point you're not going to quit, because you're in the habit.

After that, just keep adding five minutes at a time. That's about three more sets. Keep stepping it up until you're working out one hour per day. I think that's a really good long-term goal over three to five years.

## The 5 minute beginner workout

Counting reps can lead to you doing the reps too quickly and without proper form. What I recommend instead is timing your sets. That way you don't have to think about the reps, and you're not incentivized to cheat and get them done as quickly as possible.

There are many free workout timer apps you can use. The one I personally use is Gymboss. Once you have it, set it up for five 30 second rounds.

1. Bodyweight squats. Put your feet about shoulder width apart, with your weight on your heels and your toes pointed straight forward. Squat down slowly to a 90 degree angle, then go back up. If you're a beginner, try looking at the ceiling to maintain good posture as you squat.
2. Push-ups. Lower yourself down slowly until your nose is about one inch from the ground, then go back up. Keep your body flat like a plank the entire time. If you're not able to do full push-ups, put your knees on the ground and do them the same way.
3. Lunges. Take a big step forward, then dip down vertically until your knee is about one inch from the ground. Step back, repeat with the other foot, and keep going for the whole 30 seconds.
4. Towel rows. The key to [towel rows](/videos/the-back-move-you-can-do-with-a-towel/) is holding the towel tight the whole time, even when you're tired. So hold your towel taut and slowly row it back until it touches your chest. A good trick is to have a friend karate chop your towel. If the towel collapses, you don't have enough tension.
5. Rest. Jog in place for 30 seconds for an active recovery.

If you're a beginner, go through the entire circuit twice. That makes five minutes. If you're a little more advanced, you can do it three or four times. And if you're already working out one or two days a week, I recommend starting with 10 minutes.
