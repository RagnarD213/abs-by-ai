This is the setup I used to not only maintain my abs during COVID, but actually make gains, and you can get it for $58: a yoga mat, push-up handles, a jump rope and an ab wheel. Add a kettlebell, a medicine ball and dumbbells for about $94 more, about $150 in all, and that's everything I use for a complete workout at home. Keep every item cheap and basic. I'll tell you where I wasted money.

## No time or money? Neither excuse holds up

Two excuses I hear: Dan, I don't have time to go to the gym, and Dan, I don't have money for a gym membership. Those excuses are a crock of shit. You don't need a gym membership to get in shape. It's $58, and no driving back and forth to the gym.

Even with a gym membership, I still recommend this. You can't get to the gym every day. Sometimes you just need to get it in real quick. This is your emergency setup.

## The basic setup: $58

- **Yoga mat, $22**: If you only buy one thing, make it this, because most home exercises are on the floor. The bare floor works, but it's less comfortable and you get dirt on yourself. I regret the extra $10 I spent on a thick one. It's no more comfortable, it makes lunges harder and you slip around more. Thin, basic, cheap.
- **Push-up handles, $10**: I paid about $40 for rotating ones. You can just rotate the cheap ones on the ground and they do the same job. Floor push-ups work, but handles add range of motion and a more effective contraction, so push-ups get challenging again at 20, 30, 40 reps.
- **Jump rope, $9**: Excellent, quick cardio for a few dollars. I've bought expensive speed ropes and weighted ropes. Not worth the money.
- **Ab wheel, $17**: People underestimate the ab wheel because it was sold on an infomercial, but it's one of the best devices for your abs. It's continuous tension. A sit-up gives you a break at the beginning and end of each rep. The ab wheel gives you none, so it's intense even if you're already strong and have a six pack. That's [why the $17 ab wheel beats every crunch](/videos/the-17-ab-wheel-beats-every-crunch/). No need for a fancy one.

For a little over $50, you cover the basics, and this gets the job done. Even if money is tight, buy at least this.


## The intermediate setup: about $94 more

### Kettlebell, about $45

A 35 lb kettlebell is right for most beginners. Go heavier if you're advanced, lighter if you're starting out or you're a smaller person. Price scales with weight.

Pick the weight that fully exhausts you after about 30 seconds of kettlebell deadlifts, since that's the main thing we use it for in the program.

The kettlebell deadlift uses your whole body: back, legs, arms, abs, just like the regular deadlift. But it's much, much lower risk. A barbell deadlift is high risk unless your form has been trained, and most people's hasn't. That's why I never do barbell deadlifts. You can still hurt yourself with bad form, but it's much easier for beginners. Here's [how to do kettlebell deadlifts the right way at home](/videos/how-to-do-kettlebell-deadlifts-the-right-way-at-home/).

A full barbell setup at home runs $500 to $1,000 depending on the rack and plates. And when you outgrow this one for deadlifts, use it for kettlebell swings or another exercise that fits the weight.

Skip the $100 rubber-coated one. Go basic black iron.

### Medicine ball, about $20

I use it most for toe touches. Beginners don't need resistance yet. But once you can do more than 15 reps without getting tired, it keeps you in a rep range that builds ab muscle, not just cardio.

Most people buy one that's too heavy. I've trained for years and mine is only 4 lb. Lighter weight with good form gives me a better workout than heavier weight where my form goes. 6 lb is right for most people, 4 or 2 lb if you're just starting.

A 5 lb plate or dumbbell works too, so this is the place to save money. I prefer the ball, though. It's more ergonomic, doesn't slam the ground, and gives a little bounce.

### Dumbbells, $29 for a 25 lb pair

For curls, side laterals and triceps. Price scales with weight.

- **One pair:** challenging for about 30 seconds of continuous curls. About 25 lb for most people.
- **Two pairs:** add a lighter pair for 30 seconds of [side lateral raises](/videos/make-your-waist-look-smaller-with-side-lateral-raises/), in my opinion the best way to build your deltoids in a home workout. 10 lb for most men, 5 lb for most women. Err lighter, because form matters more than weight.
- **Three pairs:** add a heavy pair for about 30 seconds of overhead triceps extensions, about 40 to 45 lb. Heavy for triceps, medium for curls, light for laterals.

If you can afford a full set, buy everything up to 50 lb. You're going to get stronger.

That's about $94 more, about $150 for everything. Unless you're dead broke, buy all of it, dumbbells included. It makes a huge difference, even if you have a gym membership.


## One more thing: towels

You probably already have hand towels. But if you don't have at least five strong ones, buy a few. You'll use them for towel rows and to wipe yourself down.

## Where I wasted money so you don't have to

- A thick yoga mat. $10 extra, no difference.
- Rotating push-up handles, about $40.
- Speed ropes and weighted ropes.
- A fancy ab wheel.
- A $100 rubber-coated kettlebell.

## When to move to a gym or a home gym

Don't stay at this level your whole life. After a couple of months, get a gym membership, ideally close by so the drive is short. Barbells are the most important thing you get. You also get a leg press, a leg curl machine, and bumper plates with a platform for powerlifting moves so you don't damage the floor.

It's also a more dedicated environment. I could knock out my workout by the pool. But at my gym, with bodybuilders and fitness models around me, I get an even better workout.

If you're really committed and have the money, build a full gym at your house. I did that plus a gym membership. Both is the ultimate. So this is just my backup for when I can't make it to the gym. But it's perfectly fine to start with.
