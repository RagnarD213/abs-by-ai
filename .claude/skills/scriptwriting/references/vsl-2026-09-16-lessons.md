# Why Dan chose Claude's VSL

Updated: 2026-09-16. Scope: Dan's spoken sales copy for Abs By AI.

## The decision and evidence

Dan chose [Claude's “AI Got Me Abs at Forty”](https://claude.ai/artifact/BQ98aXCrbqX9bTfQFVzrSz)
after rejecting two Codex attempts. His final feedback:

> I still feel like Claude's writing style is better. I'm going to use that. Go ahead and analyze why Claude beat you in this task and see how you can improve in the future. I feel like this kind of has the same content as what Claude wrote about but Claude said it in a better and more persuasive way than you did

This establishes Dan's writing preference. The explanations below are an editorial
analysis of the actual passages, not measured conversion results or extra rules
Dan explicitly dictated. Claude's artifact was reread on September 16.

Comparison source: Codex's second attempt, 2,193 spoken words, at
`/Users/danielrose/.codex/visualizations/2026/09/14/01a0a22d-ab09-7a01-9a60-72c3b1017fc1/attempt-2/abs-by-ai-vsl-second-attempt.md`.
Claude's artifact reports 2,220 words. Similar total length and outline coverage
did not produce similar persuasive force. The failure was chiefly in execution.

## Paired examples

### 1. Start with an observable result

**Codex:** “The first thing AI changed was my motivation.”

**Claude:** “Number one. AI got me back in the gym.”

Both introduce motivation. Claude's verb gives the section a result immediately.
The audience can picture Dan returning to the gym. Codex requires the next
paragraph to make an abstract concept useful. Claude does the same with “AI fixed
my meal prep” and introduces the workout audit by saying it found big problems.

**Use:** Introduce what changed in someone's life, then explain the feature or
mechanism that made the change possible. This is a useful preference, not a
requirement that every paragraph use identical syntax.

### 2. Make credibility create tension

**Codex:** “I had taught millions of guys about fitness on SixPackAbs.com. And here
I was, struggling to follow my own advice.”

**Claude:** “I had taught millions of guys how to get abs. I knew exactly what to do.”
Then: “And I still let myself go.”

“About fitness” is broad; “how to get abs” is the exact outcome being sold.
Claude lets the knowledge land, then contradicts the expectation with a blunt
admission. That makes the story more interesting and makes motivation a credible
problem even for an expert. Codex compresses those beats into a summary.

**Use:** Keep the uncomfortable, concrete meaning when polishing Dan's story.
Do not turn an admission into a professional-sounding explanation. His own
admissions do not authorize accusations about the viewer.

### 3. Choose details the viewer recognises

**Codex:** “If equipment wasn't available, I could get a substitution. If I was
training at home, it could adapt the workout to what I had.”

**Claude:** “And if the squat rack is taken, or I'm stuck at home with a pair of
dumbbells, it gives me a substitution instantly.”

The content is essentially identical. Claude puts a rack and dumbbells in the
listener's mind. “Equipment,” “available,” and “adapt” make the same benefit sound
like a feature description. A concrete obstacle also gives the feature a reason
to exist. Any speed claim still needs support.

**Use:** Replace broad categories with a familiar scene when the scene clarifies
the benefit. Do not invent autobiographical events to achieve specificity.

### 4. Let the viewer recognise himself in the problem

**Codex:** “Who wants another part-time job just to eat lunch?”

**Claude:** “Normal guys like me and you were never going to do that.”

Codex's joke is reasonably vivid; it is not a bad line. Claude's passage makes a
different and useful move: Dan stands beside the prospect, explains why the old
method was impractical, then introduces the easier method. The transition builds
identification as well as describing effort. Copy needs that connection throughout,
not just an isolated clever line.

**Use:** Let Dan express a frustration the viewer already has. Show why this
method makes the next attempt more feasible, with a specific reason to believe it.

### 5. Carry the benefit through to the emotional payoff

**Codex:** “Simple. Delicious. Cheaper. And inside my calorie budget, so I kept
losing weight.”

**Claude:** “Simple to make. Actually delicious. Cheaper than what I was eating
before. And all inside my calorie budget — so I kept losing weight, without
feeling like I was on a diet.”

Codex kept the list but cut useful meaning. “To make” names the effort; “than what
I was eating before” supplies a comparison. The last phrase answers the prospect's
real objection: he expects weight loss to feel like deprivation. More words can
earn their place when they finish the sales argument.

**Use:** Judge cuts by lost meaning and desire, not word count alone. A benefit
often needs one more step: what unpleasant experience goes away, or what wanted
experience becomes possible?

### 6. Vary rhythm and build to the picture

**Codex:** “Think about taking your shirt off at the pool without spending the
whole time thinking about your stomach.”

**Claude:** “And a few months from now, you're the guy who takes his shirt off at
the pool. Not sucking it in. Not hiding. Just standing there.”

Codex explains freedom from self-consciousness. Claude acts out the physical
relief in three short beats after a longer setup. The emotional point lands at
the end. The useful craft lesson is the scene and rhythm; the specific timeline
is not automatically validated by being in the preferred script.

**Use:** Read the paragraph as speech. Longer sentences can carry setup and
motion; short ones can make an admission, reveal or payoff land. Constant fragments
sound manufactured and leave every point with the same weight.

### 7. End with the result the viewer came for

**Codex's last line:** “Let's get you a goal you're excited about — and a plan
you can finally follow.”

Claude returns to seeing himself with abs and making that change real, then
welcomes him inside. Codex finishes on a goal and a plan. Those are useful means,
but the prospect wants the body and the confidence. The ending should keep the
desired result emotionally present alongside a clear, easy next action.

**Use:** Check the final spoken beat. Does it leave the listener wanting the
outcome, or merely understanding the service? Keep the result vivid without
turning an aspiration into a guarantee.

## What went wrong in the second attempt

The revision improved coverage, clarity and some imagery, but too often restated
the same argument. It did not consistently beat the reference at the passage
level. Dan's voice was reduced to surface traits: short sentences, conversational
connectors and doubled intensifiers. Conviction, identification, contrast and
emotional consequence received less effective attention.

Some Codex passages work: the goal picture showing what Dan would get, the
ordinary-week trial, and the calorie-tracking joke. Preserve good work instead of
pretending every sentence failed. The problem is sustained persuasive strength.

The workflow also devoted substantial attention to formatting, word counts and
artifact controls. Those checks made the document usable, but did not establish
that the writing was ready. Review the spoken copy first.

## Future writing decisions

- Read a short sample of Dan's human-edited copy and this selected VSL before
  drafting substantial sales copy. Use the outline for content and the examples
  for execution; do not replace the outline or imitate its factual errors.
- For a weak passage, locate the actual missing job: a result, credible evidence,
  identification, a specific obstacle, a reveal, or an emotional payoff. “Make it
  punchier” is too vague to guide a useful revision.
- Compare corresponding opening, benefit, story and close passages as plain
  spoken copy. Identify why the revision should work better. Self-awarded scores
  and the existence of several editing passes are not proof of quality.
- When shortening, retain the phrase that resolves the objection or makes the
  result desirable. Cut generic explanation and repeated setup first.
- State supported claims directly. Keep genuine uncertainty attached to the
  relevant claim; do not weaken the surrounding voice with vague helper language.
  Do not copy “any human expert,” guaranteed motivation, scientific universals,
  unverified client results or transformation timelines merely because they sound
  forceful in the selected draft.
- Spend the main editorial effort on spoken persuasion before packaging. Judge
  improvement through Dan's future assessment and, when available, actual response
  data. Saving these lessons does not by itself demonstrate better writing.
