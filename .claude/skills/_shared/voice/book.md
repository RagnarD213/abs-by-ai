# Dan's voice: his book (real passages)

Read `../DAN-VOICE.md` first. Source: Dan's manuscript "15 Steps to Profitable YouTube Advertising", FINAL MANUSCRIPT,
Google Doc `12wKH4OUzxbcNGv40YbnBx2DBUFXuRgcW1W19OsPewdk` (2019 to 2020, about 121,000 words). Tier A: the longest pure
sample of Dan's own writing. Raw copy: `1arZ7wE67R9I8Ob3g3zt2L_OncOSMAw3GpcD8PecOhQQ`.

Three rules for using it:

- **The manuscript uses em dashes (about 4 per 1,000 words). Do not learn them.** Every passage below has each em dash
  rewritten as a comma, colon or period. The no-em-dash rule stands for everything written now. (His ad scripts from
  2019 to 2025 have none at all; he uses a spaced hyphen.)
- **Learn the voice, never the persona.** In the book Dan is an ad agency owner and a marketing coach. On the page today
  he is never a marketer or agency owner (`../WRITING-RULES.md` section 2).
- **The life details are from 2019** (overdrawn nine years earlier, the studio apartment and bicycle, the trainer
  partner, the sale of the company, the yacht joke). Never lift one into a script. Current facts come only from memory
  `dan-personal-facts-for-scripts`.

## B1. Story opener: the body feels it first

Intro, "Nine Years Ago on This Day...".

> A sick feeling of fear surged through my body as I stared at my bank account balance on the screen. I stopped breathing momentarily. My heart started beating faster in panic. I was overdrawn, again.
>
> How was I going to pay our rent at the end of the month?
>
> I lowered my head onto my computer desk in defeat, ready to give up.

Why: physical sensation, a short verdict sentence ("I was overdrawn, again."), the question he asked himself, then a
one-line paragraph of defeat. No scene-setting, no date line, no "picture this".

## B2. The turn: one question, a two-word answer

Intro, "My Present Day Reality".

> Fast forward nine years later, and this moment barely seems real to me.
>
> What changed everything for me?
>
> In two words: YouTube Marketing.

Why: the payoff of the whole story is held for one beat and named plainly. Same move as his ads' "Shockingly, the
thing that changed everything for me was…AI." (`ads.md`).

## B3. Fear, then the bigger fear, then the reader's advantage

Intro.

> I personally wasted hundreds of thousands of dollars on unprofitable YouTube campaigns in my early years. And if you start advertising on YouTube without knowing what you're doing, you are going to lose a lot of money, fast.
>
> But losing money will actually be the LEAST of your problems...
>
> But by investing in your YouTube advertising skills through this book, you've given yourself a HUGE advantage.

Why: his own loss first (that is the credential), then the reader's loss, then an escalation line with a capitalized
word and an ellipsis, then a "But" paragraph that hands the reader the win.

## B4. Naming the reader's worry before he raises it

Step 1, Mistake #2.

> Now, I know this might sound discouraging if you are just starting off and are nowhere near the most popular channel in your market.
>
> The brutal truth is that you can't. It's impossible.
>
> You should give up on this losing strategy. Instead, use the same approach that I successfully used in the early days of the Six Pack Shortcuts channel.

Why: "Now, I know…" names the worry. The answer is blunt and short. Then the alternative, proven by what he did.

## B5. Provocation, then the myth, then a real person

Step 1 Mistake #1 and the Step 2 opener.

> I'm going to piss a lot of people off with this one, but it's true, and it needs to be said.

> "Sell what you love, and the money will follow."
>
> This is the most dangerous and the most destructive myth in business. And it's a guaranteed recipe for failure with YouTube marketing.
>
> We'd all like to believe that if we're passionate enough about something, we can make money from selling it. But a decade of experience with my own companies, and consulting with many different clients, has shown me this simply isn't true.

Why: he warns he is about to say something unpopular, then says it in the strongest words. He includes himself in the
comfortable belief ("We'd all like to believe") before knocking it down. A topic swap (a fitness myth) uses the same
shape.

## Patterns measured across the whole book

- Sentences run medium to long (about 19 words on average) and paragraphs are mostly one or two sentences: a sales
  letter on the page. Very short sentences are saved for turns ("It's impossible.", "In two words: YouTube Marketing.").
- "you" is about 1 word in 18. Rhetorical questions are rare (about 1 per 1,000 words) and always answered on the next
  line.
- ALL-CAPS on one stressed word (FAR, HUGE, ONLY, NOT), about 7 per 1,000 words. Ellipses as spoken pauses mid-sentence.
- Pet words: shockingly, tremendously, killer, dialed in, "go all in", rule of thumb, "For example", "Let's say",
  "That's why", "I recommend", "I'll show you exactly".
- Chapters end by crediting the reader, naming the next payoff and a short push: "So keep reading. We're just about to
  get into the good stuff."
